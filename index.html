research

I study how pedestrians and autonomous vehicles negotiate shared space when there is no driver to make eye contact with. My doctoral work introduces the Smart Pole Interaction Unit (SPIU): an infrastructure-based external HMI that decouples intent signaling from the vehicle itself, delivering explicit "WALK" and "STOP" cues to pedestrians via elevated, context-aware visual signals. The work has been evaluated in VR with N=40 participants across Japan and Norway, and in-the-wild with a mobile SPIU on a real autonomous vehicle. In parallel I contribute to the Autoware open-source stack at Tier IV, where the same software runs both my experiments and real vehicles on the road.

Selected research threads:

Infrastructure-based external HMI

Conventional eHMI lives on the vehicle. SPIU moves the channel into the built environment — a pole at the curb that knows what the approaching vehicle will do and signals it in place. We've shown this reduces pedestrian decision time by 40% to over 80% compared to a no-SPIU baseline, depending on scenario and cultural context. The work spans early VR prototypes through to in-the-wild deployment of a mobile SPIU with cameras and LED displays, evaluated against a four-factor design (CarBehavior × Mobility × eHMI × SPIU) showing significant gains in understandability, trust, and perceived safety.

Cross-cultural pedestrian behaviour

Most eHMI research happens in one country; deployment is global. During a visit to NTNU Norway I ran a parallel VR study (40 participants, 32 trials each) replicating an experiment originally developed in Japan. Results showed SPIU's adaptability across differing urban and social contexts, with effect sizes varying by culture in informative ways.

Synthetic participants for HCI

Field studies are irreplaceable but slow and expensive. In collaboration with lab members, I co-authored work asking whether Vision-Language Model personas can stand in for real participants in pedestrian–AV interaction studies. We ran parallel experiments with 20 real participants and 20 VLM personas and found that VLMs capture key behavioural patterns but lack nuance in non-verbal cues and complex social behaviour — a useful limit on the "synthetic participant" idea now circulating in HCI.

Reproducible simulation for HCI research

Together with collaborators I maintain an open VR + AWSIM testbed so that pedestrian–AV studies can actually be replicated. The same scenarios drive both VR experiments and physical-robot deployments.

about

I am a third-year doctoral candidate (D3) in Creative Informatics at The University of Tokyo, working in Tsukada Laboratory under Prof. Manabu Tsukada. Before that I did a master's at the same university on the Todai Fellowship, with a research focus that gradually narrowed from broad robotics into the specific question of how humans and autonomous vehicles communicate without language.

Earlier in my path I read information technology at Vel Tech University with exchange research at NTU Singapore; held research internships at RIKEN, ispace, and National Chung Cheng University in Taiwan; and was a visiting researcher at NTNU Norway. Alongside the PhD I work part-time at Tier IV as an Autonomous Driving Engineer on the Autoware Universe stack.
